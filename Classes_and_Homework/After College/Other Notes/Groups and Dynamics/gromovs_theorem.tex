This is going to be a broad overview of the ideas and techniques used to prove the following much celebrated theorem.
\begin{theorem}[Gromov's Theorem on Groups of Polynomial Growth]
  If a finitely generated group $\Gamma$ has polynomial growth, then $\Gamma$ contains a nilpotent subgroup of finite index.
\end{theorem}
All of this work is discussed in Gromov's paper and the paper by van den Dries and Wilkie, and I am only writing this up for personal use (and to present to the University of Virginia Graduate Students Seminar).
\section{Necessary Algebraic Results}%
\begin{definition}
  Suppose $\Gamma$ has polynomial growth. We will let $\operatorname{growth}\left( \Gamma \right)$ be the smallest natural number $d$ such that there exists $C$ such that $\left\vert B\left( r \right) \right\vert\leq Cr^{d}$ for each $r\geq 1$. Here, $B\left( r \right)$ denotes the closed unit ball of radius $r$ centered at the identity.
\end{definition}
Suppose $\Gamma'\leq \Gamma$ is a subgroup of a finitely generated group acting via translation. Let $X = \Gamma/\Gamma'$ be the quotient space, and let $f\colon \Gamma\to X$ the natural projection. Let $\dist\left( x,y \right) = \inf_{\alpha,\beta}d\left( \alpha,\beta \right)$ for any $\alpha\in f^{-1}\left( x \right)$ and $\beta\in f^{-1}\left( y \right)$. Since $\Gamma'$ acts via isometries on $\Gamma$, it follows that $\dist\left( x,y \right)$ is a metric on $X$.

Observe that for any two points $x,y\in X$ with $\dist\left( x,y \right) = p$ for some integer $p$, there exist points $x = x_0,x_1,\dots,x_p=y$ such that $\dist\left( x_i,x_{i-1} \right) = 1$. Importantly, this means that if $X$ is infinite, then $\left\vert B_X(x,r) \right\vert\geq r+1$ for all $x\in X$ and all $r\geq 0$.
\begin{lemma}[Splitting Lemma]
  If $\Gamma'\subseteq \Gamma$ is a \textit{finitely generated} subgroup of \textit{infinite} index. Then, $\operatorname{growth}\left( \Gamma' \right)\leq \operatorname{growth}\left( \Gamma \right)-1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Since $\Gamma/\Gamma'$ is connected, it follows that $B(r)$ for each $r\in \Gamma$ contains at least $r+1$ elements $\alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_r\in \Gamma$ such that $f\left( \alpha_i \right)\neq f\left( \alpha_j \right)$ whenever $i\neq j$, where $f\colon \Gamma\to \Gamma/\Gamma'$ is the quotient map. The intersections $B' = B(r)\cap \Gamma'$ and the translates $B'\alpha_i$ are disjoint, and they are contained in $B\left( 2r \right)$, so that $\left\vert B\left( 2r \right) \right\vert\geq \left( r+1 \right)\left\vert B' \right\vert$.
\end{proof}
The following theorems are especially important in the algebraic proof. We will not prove them here.
\begin{theorem}[Jordan's Theorem]
  For any Lie group $L$ with finitely many components, there is a number $q$ such that every finite subgroup in $L$ contains an abelian subgroup of index at most $q$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Tits Alternative]
  Let $L$ be a Lie group with finitely many components, and let $G\subseteq L$ be an arbitrary finitely generated subgroup. Then, either
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $G$ has a free group of rank $2$ (hence is of exponential growth);
    \item $G$ is virtually nilpotent (hence $G$ has exponential growth unless it is virtually nilpotent).
  \end{itemize}
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Milnor--Wolf Theorem]
  If $\Gamma$ is a virtually solvable group, then either $\Gamma$ is of exponential growth or $\Gamma$ is virtually nilpotent.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
  Given a nilpotent group $G$ with lower central series
  \begin{align*}
    G = \gamma_1(G) \geq \gamma_2(G)\geq\cdots\geq \gamma_c(G)\geq \gamma_{c+1}(G) = \set{e_G},
  \end{align*}
  the Bass--Guivarch formula gives that $G$ grows polynomially with degree given by
  \begin{align*}
    d &= \sum_{i=1}^{c}i\operatorname{rk}\left( \gamma_i(G)/\gamma_{i+1}(G) \right),
  \end{align*}
  where $\operatorname{rk}$ denotes the rank of the abelian groups $\gamma_i(G)/\gamma_{i+1}(G)$.
\end{remark}
\begin{lemma}[Lemma (a)]
  Let $L$ be a Lie group with finitely many components, and suppose that for each $p=1,2,\dots$, there is a homomorphism $G\to L$ such that its image is finite and has at least $p$ elements. Then, $G$ contains a subgroup $G'\subseteq G$ of finite index such that $\left[ G',G' \right]\subseteq G'$ has infinite index.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  We start by observing that if $G$ is a finitely generated group with finite generating set $S$, then given any index $k$, $G$ has finitely many subgroups of index $k$. This follows from the fact that if $H$ is any such subgroup, then the action of $G$ on $G/H$ yields a homomorphism $G\to S_k$ (and so $H$ is determined by the given homomorphism), of which there are at most $\left( k! \right)^{\left\vert S \right\vert}$ such homomorphisms.

  Therefore, there are finitely many subgroups of index at most $q$, and since the finite intersection of finite index subgroups is finite index, it follows that if we let
  \begin{align*}
    G' &\coloneq \bigcap\set{H\leq G : \left[ G:H \right]\leq q},
  \end{align*}
  then $G'$ is of finite index.

  We will show that $\left[ G',G' \right]$ is of infinite index by showing that $G'$ admits a surjection onto abelian subgroups of arbitrary size. If $\phi\colon G\to L$ has image $\left\vert F_p \right\vert\geq p$, then $G'\subseteq \phi^{-1}\left( A_p \right) $, where $A_p\leq F_p$ is abelian and has index $q$. Since
  \begin{align*}
    \left[ F_p:\phi\left( G' \right) \right] &\leq \left[ G:G' \right],
  \end{align*}
  it follows that
  \begin{align*}
    \left\vert \phi\left( G' \right) \right\vert &\geq \frac{p}{\left[ G:G' \right]}.
  \end{align*}
  Since $p$ is arbitrary, it follows that $\left[ G',G' \right]\subseteq G'$ has infinite index.
\end{proof}
\begin{lemma}[Lemma (b)]
  Suppose $\Gamma$ is a finitely generated group of polynomial growth. Then, $\left[ \Gamma,\Gamma \right]$ is finitely generated.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  It suffices to show that the kernel $\Delta\subseteq \Gamma$ of any homomorphism $g\colon \Gamma\to\Z$ is finitely generated. 

  Let $\gamma_0,\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_k\in \Gamma$ be a system of generators such that $g\left( \gamma_0 \right) = 1\in \Z$ and $\gamma_i\in \Delta$ for each $i=1,\dots,k$. Let
  \begin{align*}
    \Delta_m &= \left\langle \set{\gamma_0^{j}\gamma_i\gamma_0^{-j} : 1\leq i \leq k, -m\leq j \leq m} \right\rangle.
  \end{align*}
  Then,
  \begin{align*}
    \Delta &= \bigcup_{m=0}^{\infty} \Delta_m.
  \end{align*}
  If the sequence of $\Delta_m$ stabilizes, then it equals $\Delta$ and we are done. Else, there are $\alpha_m\in \Delta$ such that each $\alpha_m$ is of the form $\alpha_m = \gamma_0^{m}\gamma_i\gamma_0^{-m}$ or $\alpha_m = \gamma_0^{-m}\gamma_i\gamma_0^{m}$ satisfying $\alpha_m\notin \left\langle \alpha_0,\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_{m-1} \right\rangle$.

  Were such a family to exist, our goal is to show that it implies the existence of an exponentially growing family. Let $\beta\left( \epsilon_0,\dots,\epsilon_m \right) = \alpha_0^{\epsilon_0}\cdots\alpha_m^{\epsilon_m}$, where $\epsilon_i \in\set{0,1}$ for each $i$. Since equality between any two such elements $\beta$ is defined by equality of each $\epsilon_i$, it follows that there are $2^{m+1}$ such $\beta$.

  Notice then that $\ell\left( \beta \right)\leq \sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell\left( \alpha_m \right)\leq \left( m+1 \right)\left( 2m+1 \right)$. Thus, $\left\vert B\left( \left( m+1 \right)\left( 2m+1 \right) \right) \right\vert\leq 2^{m+1}$ as the ball of radius $\left( m+1 \right)\left( 2m+1 \right)$ has all the $\beta$. Yet, this contradicts polynomial growth.
\end{proof}
The most important applications of these are to prove the following lemma. 
\begin{lemma}[Lemma 1]
  Let $\Gamma$ be a finitely generated group of polynomial growth, and let $L$ be a Lie group with finitely many connected components. Suppose that for each finitely generated infinite group $\Gamma'\subseteq \Gamma$, there is a subgroup $\Delta\subseteq \Gamma'$ of finite index such that, for all $p=1,2,\dots$, there is a homomorphism $\Delta\to L$ such that its image contains at least $p$ elements.

  Then, $\Gamma$ is virtually nilpotent.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  By the splitting lemma, we inductively assume that all finitely generated subgroups of $\Gamma$ with infinite index are virtually nilpotent.

  Let $\Delta\subseteq \Gamma$ be a subgroup of finite index with the required homomorphisms into $L$. If these homomorphisms are all finite, Lemma (a) yields a subgroup $\Delta'\subseteq \Delta$ with finite index such that $\left[ \Delta',\Delta' \right]\subseteq \Delta$ has infinite index. Induction yields the final result in this case.

  If there is a homomorphism $\Delta\to L$ with infinite image, then the Tits theorem applied to this image yields a finite index subgroup $\Delta'\subseteq \Delta$ such that $\left[ \Delta',\Delta' \right]\subseteq \Delta$ is infinite index as well. According to Lemma (b), the commutator $\left[ \Delta',\Delta' \right]$ is finitely generated, so by the induction hypothesis, it is virtually nilpotent. Therefore, $\Gamma$ is virtually solvable, so by the Milnor--Wolf Theorem, it follows that $\Gamma$ is virtually nilpotent.
\end{proof}
\section{Notions from Nonstandard Analysis}%
In Gromov's original paper, he constructed a special metric space $Y$ as a subsequential limit of the sequence of discrete metric spaces $\left( \Gamma,\left( 1/n \right)d_S \right)$, where $S$ is a generating set for $S$. The metric space $Y$ has the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item $Y$ is homogeneous;
  \item $Y$ is connected and locally connected;
  \item $Y$ is complete;
  \item $Y$ is locally compact and of finite topological dimension.
\end{enumerate}
The deep result of Gleason--Montgomery--Zippin gives that $\operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$ is then a connected Lie group. There is then a finite-index subgroup $\Gamma'\subseteq \Gamma$ that admits a homomorphism $\Gamma'\to \operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$ such that the image of $\Gamma'$ has arbitrary order, so we may use Lemma 1 to obtain the desired result.
\begin{definition}
  Given a metric space $Y$ with a distinguished point $e$, $\operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$ is topologized by defining the basic closed neighborhoods
  \begin{align*}
    B_{k,\ve} &= \set{\sigma\in \operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right) : k\in \Z_{>0}\text{ and }\ve > 0\text{ with }d\left( \sigma(y),y \right) \leq \ve\text{ for all $y$ with }d\left( y,e \right)\leq k}
  \end{align*}
  at the identity.
\end{definition}
The way we will construct this is through the asymptotic cone. We will use the construction in \cite{van_den_dries_wilkie_paper} that involves methods from nonstandard analysis. 

Let $\R$ denote the real numbers, and let $ \mathcal{U} $ be an ultrafilter on $\N$. On the set $\R^{\N}$, we may define an equivalence relation by saying two sequences $\left( u_n \right)_n$ and $\left( v_n \right)_n$ are equivalent if
\begin{align*}
  \set{n\in \N : u_n = v_n}&\in \mathcal{U}.
\end{align*}
We denote by $\R^{\mathcal{U}}$, which will be written as $\R^{\ast}$, the ultrapower of $\R$. It c an be shown that the operations that turn $\R$ into an ordered field, such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and the ordering on $\R$ admit extensions to $\R^{\ast}$ that turn $\R^{\ast}$ into an ordered field. We will identify $\R\subseteq \R^{\ast}$ by taking $s\mapsto \left[ s \right]$, where $\left[ s \right]$ is the equivalence class of the constant sequence with value $s$.

In particular, we define
\begin{align*}
  \left[ \left( u_n \right)_n \right]\pm \left[ \left( u_n \right)_n \right] &= \left[ \left( u_n \pm v_n \right)_n \right]\\
  \left[ \left( u_n \right)_n \right] \left[ \left( v_n \right)_n \right] &= \left[ \left( u_nv_n \right)_n \right]\\
  \left[ \left( u_n \right)_n \right]/\left[ \left( v_n \right)_n \right] &= \begin{cases}
    u_n/v_n & v_n\neq 0\\
    0 & v_n = 0,
  \end{cases}
\end{align*}
and we say that $\left[ \left( u_n \right)_n \right]\leq \left[ \left( v_n \right)_n \right]$ if and only if $\set{n : u_n\leq v_n}\in \mathcal{U}$.

We note that the nonstandard construction does not preserve the Archimedean property. In particular, we may define an element $\left[ u \right]$ to be \textit{finite} if there exists $s\in \R$ with $s > 0$ such that $\left[ -s \right]\leq \left[ u \right]\leq \left[ s \right]$, and we say the element is \textit{infinite} if it is not finite. We say the element is \textit{infinitesimal} if for all $s\in \R$ with $s > 0$, we have $\left[ -s \right]\leq \left[ u \right]\leq \left[ s \right]$. If $\R^{\operatorname{fin}}$ denotes the finite elements of $\R^{\ast}$ and $\R^{0}$ denotes the infinitesimal elements, then there is an isomorphism $\rho\colon \R\to \R^{\operatorname{fin}}/\R^{0}$; if $h\colon \R\to \R^{\operatorname{fin}}$ is the natural inclusion map, then the homomorphism $\operatorname{st} = \rho^{-1}\circ h$ from $\R^{\operatorname{fin}}$ to $\R^{0}$ is called the \textit{standard part} map.
\begin{definition}
  Let $S$ be any set, $\omega$ an ultrafilter on $\N$, and let $S^{\omega}$ be the corresponding ultrapower. That is, $S^{\omega} = S^{\N}/\sim_{\omega}$, where $f,g\in S^{\N}$ are equal if one has $\set{i\in \N : f(i) = g(i)}\in \omega$. Identify $S\subseteq S^{\omega}$ with the equivalence classes of their respective constant functions.

  Given sets $S_1,\dots,S_n$, a subset $W\subseteq S_1^{\omega}\times\cdots\times S_n^{\omega}$ is called \textit{internal} if it is realized by coordinates. That is, for each $k\in \N$, there is $W_k\subseteq S_1\times\cdots\times S_n$ such that for all $f_1\in S_1^{\N},\dots,f_n\in S_n^{\N}$, we have that
  \begin{align*}
    \left( \left[ f_1 \right],\dots,\left[ f_n \right] \right) &\in W
      \intertext{if and only if}
    \left( f_1(k),\dots,f_n(k) \right) &\in W_k\text{ for a.e. $k$.}
  \end{align*}
  Similarly, a map $F\colon S_1^{\omega}\times\cdots\times S_{n-1}^{\omega}\to S_n^{\omega}$ is called internal if for all $k\in \N$, there exists $F_k\colon S_1\times\cdots\times S_{n-1}\to S_n$ such that for all $f_1\in S_1^{\N},\dots,f_{n-1}\in S_{n-1}^{\N}$, we have that
  \begin{align*}
    F\left( \left[ f_1 \right],\dots,\left[ f_{n-1} \right] \right) &= \left[ \left( F_k\left( f_1(k),\dots,f_{n-1}(k) \right) \right)_{k} \right]
  \end{align*}
\end{definition}
Notice that internal subsets are closed under intersections, unions, and set subtraction.

We will need the following lemmas related to internal subsets and functions on the nonstandard naturals.
\begin{lemma}
  Suppose $g_n\in S^{\omega}$ for each $n\in \N$. Then, there is an internal function $F\colon \N^{\ast}\to S^{\omega}$ such that $F(n) = g_n$ for all $n\in \N$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Let $g_n = \left[ f_n \right]$ for some function $f\in S^{\N}$. For each $i\in \N$, let $F_i\colon \N\to S$ be defined by $F_i(n) = f_n(i)$. Then, we define $F\colon \N^{\ast}\to S^{\omega}$ to be the map with components $\set{F_i : i\in \N}$. For any $n\in \N$, we identify $\hat{n}\in \N^{\ast}$ with the constant sequence. Then, we have
  \begin{align*}
    F\left( \left[ \left( \hat{n}(i) \right)_{i\in \N} \right] \right) &= \left[ \left( F_i\left( \hat{n}(i) \right) \right)_{i\in \N} \right]\\
                                                                       &= \left[ \left( F_i(n) \right)_{i\in \N} \right]\\
                                                                       &= \left[ \left( f_n(i) \right)_{i\in \N} \right]\\
                                                                       &= g_n.
  \end{align*}
\end{proof}
\begin{lemma}
  Suppose $A\subseteq \N^{\ast}$ is an internal subset. Then, $A$ has a least element.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Write $\left( A_n \right)_{n\in \N}$ for the components of $A$. Then, the equivalence class of the sequence $\left( \min\left( A_n \right) \right)_{n\in \N}$ is the smallest element of $A$.
\end{proof}
\begin{lemma}[Overspill Lemma]
  Suppose $X\subseteq \N^{\ast}$ is an internal subset, and $k\in \N$. If $n\in X$ for all $n\in \N$ with $n\geq k$, then there is an infinite $K\in \N^{\ast}$ with $n\in X$ for all $n\in \N^{\ast}$ with $k\leq n \leq K$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Suppose it were the case that all infinite nonstandard naturals were in $K$. Then, any infinite $K\in \N^{\ast}$ is satisfactory.

  Else, there are infinite nonstandard naturals not in $X$. Our goal is now to show that there is a smallest such infinite nonstandard natural not in $X$. It would then be the case that all infinite nonstandard naturals smaller than $H$ will be in $X$.

  We observe that the set
  \begin{align*}
    Y &= \set{n\in \N^{\ast} : k < n\in \N^{\ast}\setminus X}
  \end{align*}
  is then nonempty, and $Y$ is internal since $Y$ is given by
  \begin{align*}
    \left( \N^{\ast}\setminus \set{1,\dots,k} \right)\cap \left( \N^{\ast}\setminus X \right).
  \end{align*}
  Therefore, $Y$ has a least element $H$. So, $H$ is a nonstandard natural greater than $k$ but not in $X$; since all finite $n\geq k$ are in $X$, it follows that $H$ is infinite. Furthermore, $K = H-1$ then is infinite and satisfies the requirements of the theorem.
\end{proof}
The Los theorem says it is possible to show that a first order sentence $\Phi^{\omega}$ in elements and internal subsets in the ultrapowers $S_1^{\omega}\times\cdots\times S_n^{\omega}$ if and only if the underlying first order sentence $\Phi$ holds for almost every $i\in \N$. In fact, if $\Phi$ is an elementary statement (i.e., expressed entirely in elements), then $\Phi^{\ast}$ expresses the same property as $\Phi$.
\section{Constructing the Asymptotic Cone $Y$}%
Let $\omega$ be an ultrafilter on $\N$. Then, analogous to how we constructed $\R^{\ast}$, we are able to construct the ultrapower $\Gamma^{\omega}$ by taking $\Gamma^{\omega}/\sim_{\omega}$. We note that if $S$ is a finite generating set for $\Gamma$, then there is an elementary statement that says ``$S$ generates $\Gamma$,'' so there is a nonstandard extension. Every element $g\in \Gamma^{\omega}$ may then be written as a word in the generators, but the length of the word may be a nonstandard natural number (i.e., it may be infinite); since the set of all $n\in \N^{\ast}$ for which such a word of length $n$ exists is an internal subset (for fixed $g$), it follows that there is a nonstandard length function, which we will denote $\ell_{\omega}$.

Let $R\in \R^{\ast}$ be an infinite nonstandard real. Define
\begin{align*}
  \Gamma^{(R)} &= \set{g\in \Gamma^{\omega} : \text{there exists $A > 0$ in $\R$ such that }\frac{\ell_{\omega}\left( g \right)}{R} \leq A}
\end{align*}
as a subgroup of $\Gamma^{\omega}$. Let
\begin{align*}
  N &= \set{g\in \Gamma^{\omega} : \frac{\ell_{\omega}\left( g \right)}{R}\text{ is infinitesimal}}
\end{align*}
be the subgroup of infinitesimal elements of $\Gamma^{(R)}$. Then, $\ell_{\omega}\left( \cdot \right)/R$ defines a map from $\Gamma^{(R)}$ to $\R^{\operatorname{fin}}$, and $\ell_{\omega}\left( g \right)/R-\ell_{\omega}\left( h \right)/R$ is infinitesimal whenever $gN = hN$ in the coset space $\Gamma^{(R)}/N$. Therefore, the map $\ell_{\omega}$ descends to a map $ \overline{\ell}\colon \Gamma^{(R)}/N\to \R $ defined by
\begin{align*}
  \overline{\ell}\left( gN \right) &= \operatorname{st}\left( \ell_{\omega}\left( g \right)/R \right).
\end{align*}
Let $Y = \Gamma^{(R)}/N$. Then, $Y$ is equipped with the metric $ \overline{d} $ given by $ \overline{d}\left( gN,hN \right) = \overline{\ell}\left( h^{-1}gN \right) $.
\begin{proposition}
  The metric space $Y$ is
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item homogeneous;
    \item connected and locally connected (by geodesics);
    \item complete.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}\hfill
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item The group $\Gamma^{(R)}$ acts on $Y$ from the left by isometries, with for all $a\in \Gamma^{(R)}$ having
      \begin{align*}
        \overline{d}\left( agN,ahN \right) &= \overline{\ell}\left( h^{-1}a^{-1}agN \right)\\
                                           &= \overline{\ell}\left( h^{-1}gN \right)\\
                                           &= \overline{d}\left( gN,hN \right).
      \end{align*}
      This action is transitive. Thus, $Y$ is homogeneous.
    \item Without loss of generality, assume $p = eN$ and $q = gN$. Furthermore, we may assume without loss of generality that $ \overline{\ell}\left( gN \right) = 1 $ (extending by concatenating paths), meaning that $\ell_{\omega}\left( g \right) = R$. We may extend the floor function $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor$ to $\R^{\ast}$. We may then write $g$ as a representation 
      \begin{align*}
        g &= g_1\cdots g_{\lfloor R \rfloor}
      \end{align*}
      as a nonstandard word. The map $f\colon [0,1]\to Y$ given by $f(t) = g_1\cdots g_{\lfloor tR\rfloor}$ is then continuous.
    \item Suppose $\left( g_nN \right)_{n\in \N}$ is a Cauchy sequence in $Y$. Assume that this Cauchy sequence is bounded by $1$. Therefore, we have that $ \ell_{\omega}\left( g \right) \leq R $.

      We may extend $\left( g_n \right)_{n\in \N}$ to an internal sequence $\left( g_n \right)_{n\in \N^{\ast}}$. For each strictly positive (standard) natural number $k$, let $M(k)\in \N$ be such that $\ell_{\omega}\left( g_m^{-1}g_n \right) < R/k$ for all (standard) naturals $m,n\geq M(k)$. This may be extended to all nonstandard natural numbers $m,n\in \N^{\ast}$ bounded above by some infinite $N(k)\in \N^{\ast}$. This follows from the application of the above argument to the internal set
      \begin{align*}
        \set{t\in \N^{\ast} : \text{ for all $m,n\in \N^{\ast}$, one has that $M(k)< m,n < t$ implies that $\ell_{\omega}\left( g_m^{-1}g_n \right) < R/k$}}.
      \end{align*}
      So, there is some $\theta\in \N^{\ast}$ such that $\theta\geq M(k)$ and $\theta \leq N(k)$ for all standard natural numbers $k > 0$. Thus, we have $\lim_{n}g_nN = g_{\theta}N$ in $Y$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}
It is important to note that the space $Y$ depends on the choice of nonstandard real $R$ that is used to define it. However, it is not dependent on the choice of generating set, since any two choices of generating sets admits a bilipschitz equivalence (by writing generators in one set through generators in the other set).
\section{Local Compactness and Finite-Dimensionality of $Y$}%
From now on, we will write $d$ for the distance metric on $Y$ and $\ell$ for the length function on $\Gamma^{\omega}$.
\begin{lemma}
  Let $R_0$ be a positive infinite nonstandard real, and suppose $d$ is such that $\left\vert B_{Y}\left( e,R_0 \right) \right\vert \leq cR_0^{d}$ for some positive finite real number $c$ and some natural $d\in \N$.

  Then, there is a positive infinite $S\leq R_0$ such that for every $i\in \N$ with $i\geq 4$, the following property denoted $P_i(S)$ holds: if $g_1,\dots,g_t\in B_{Y}\left( e,S/4 \right)$ for some $t\in \N^{\ast}$, and $B_{Y}\left( g_1,S/i \right),\dots,B_Y\left( g_t,S/i \right)$ are pairwise disjoint, then $t\leq i^{d+1}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Suppose the lemma is false. Then, for all $S\in \R^{\ast}$ with $\log\left( R_0 \right)\leq S \leq R_0$, there is some $i\geq 4$ in $\N$ such that $P_i(S)$ fails.

  The map $S\to \min\set{i\in \N : P_i(S)\text{ fails}}$ is an internal function whose range is in the naturals, so it is finite and hence bounded by some $K\in \N$.

  By internal induction, we may define natural numbers $4\leq i_1,\dots,i_u\leq K$ for some $u\in \N^{\ast}$ and elements $g(l,j)\in \Gamma^{\omega}$, where $1\leq l \leq u$, $1\leq j \leq \lfloor i_l^{d+1}\rfloor + 1$, such that
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $g(l,j)\in B_Y\left( e,\frac{R_0}{4i_1\cdots i_{l-1}} \right)$, and
    \item $B_Y\left( g(l,j),\frac{R_0}{i_1\cdots i_{l-1} i_{l}} \right)\cap B_Y\left( g(l,j'), \frac{R_0}{i_1\cdots i_{l-1}i_{l}}\right) = \emptyset$ for each $1\leq j < j' \leq t_l$.
  \end{itemize}
  We note that this inductive definition of $i_l$ and $g\left( l,j \right)$ may continue as long as the condition $P_i\left( R_0/\left( i_1\cdots i_{l-1} \right) \right)$ is not satisfied. Such a family will be guaranteed to exist if we choose $u$ such that
  \begin{align*}
    \frac{R_0}{i_1\cdots i_{u-1}} \geq \log\left( R_0 \right) > \frac{R_0}{i_1\cdots i_{u-1}i_u}.
  \end{align*}
  Let
  \begin{align*}
    T &= \set{\left( s_1,\dots,s_u \right) : s_l\in \N,1\leq s_l\leq t_l\text{ for each }l=1,\dots,u}.
  \end{align*}
  For any $s = \left( s_1,\dots,s_u \right)\in T$, define
  \begin{align*}
    g_s &= g\left( 1,s_1 \right)g\left( 2,s_2 \right)\cdots g\left( u,s_u \right),
  \end{align*}
  which satisfies
  \begin{align*}
    \ell\left( g_s \right) &\leq \sum_{l=1}^{u} \ell\left( g\left( l,s_l \right) \right)\\
                           &\leq \sum_{l=1}^{u} \frac{R_0}{4i_1\cdots i_{l-1}}\\
                           &\leq \sum_{l=1}^{u} \frac{R_0}{4^{l}}\\
                           &< R_0.
  \end{align*}
  Therefore, we have the containment $\set{g_s : s\in T}\subseteq B\left( e,R_0 \right)$. If $s\neq s'$ in $T$, then we claim that $g_s = g_{s'}$. If not, then there is some $v < u$ such that 
  \begin{align*}
    g\left( v,s_v \right)\cdots g\left( u,s_u \right) = g\left( v,s_v' \right)\cdots g\left( u,s_u' \right)
  \end{align*}
  where $s_v \neq s_v'$. Then, it follows that
  \begin{align*}
    g\left( v,s_v' \right)^{-1}g\left( v,s_v \right) &= g\left( v+1,s_{v+1}' \right)\cdots g_{u,s_u'}g\left( u,s_u \right)^{-1}\cdots g\left( v+1,s_{v+1} \right)^{-1}.
  \end{align*}
  Yet, this means that
  \begin{align*}
    \frac{R_0}{i_1\cdots i_{v}} &\leq \ell\left( g\left( v,s_v' \right)^{-1}g\left( v,s_v \right) \right)\\
                                &\leq 2\sum_{l=v+1}^{u} \frac{R_0}{4i_1\cdots i_{l-1}}\\
                                &\leq \frac{R_0}{2i_1\cdots i_v}\left( 1 + \sum_{l=v+2}^{u}\frac{1}{4^{l-v-1}} \right)\\
                                & < \frac{R_0}{i_1\cdots i_v}.
  \end{align*}
  Thus, $g_s\neq g_{s'}$ for any $s\neq s'$. Yet, this means that
  \begin{align*}
    \left\vert \set{g_s : s\in T} \right\vert &= \left\vert T \right\vert\\
                                              &\geq \prod_{l=1}^{u} i_l^{d+1}\\
                                              &> \left( R_0/\log\left( R_0 \right) \right)^{d+1}\\
                                              &> cR_0^{d},
  \end{align*}
  where the latter inequality emerges from the fact that $R_0$ is infinite. Thus, we have that $\left\vert B\left( e,R_0 \right) \right\vert > cR_0^{d}$, yielding our desired contradiction.
\end{proof}
Now we will briefly discuss topological dimension. Let $p\in \R$ be any positive real number, and let $\ve > 0$. Suppose $X$ is a topological space. We let
\begin{align*}
  H_p^{\delta}(X) &= \inf\set{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left( \operatorname{diam}\left( U_j \right) \right)^{p} : X = \bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}U_j\text{ and }\operatorname{diam}\left( U_j \right) < \delta\text{ for each }j}.
\end{align*}
Then, we let
\begin{align*}
  H_p\left( X \right) &= \sup_{\delta > 0} H_p^{\delta}\left( X \right).
\end{align*}
This is the \textit{Hausdorff content} of $X$. We define the \textit{dimension} of $X$ to be the supremum over all $p\geq 0$ such that $H_p(X) > 0$.

For the following proposition, we will let $Y^{(R)}$ denote the space $\Gamma^{(R)}/N$ from earlier.
\begin{proposition}
  Suppose $\operatorname{growth}\left( \Gamma \right)\leq d$ for some $d\in \N$. Then, there exists some positive infinite $R\in \R^{\ast}$ such that the metric space $Y^{(R)}$ is locally compact with (Hausdorff) dimension $d\leq 1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  By the overspill principle, there is some positive infinite $R_0\in \R^{\ast}$ such that $\left\vert B\left( e,R_0 \right) \right\vert\leq cR_0^{d}$. Let $S\in \R^{\ast}$ be such that the property $P_i(S)$ from the previous lemma holds. Set $R = S/4$. We will show that $Y^{(R)}$ satisfies the necessary conditions.

  Let $k\in \N$ be strictly positive. In particular, by the way that $P_i(S)$ is defined, we have that for any $g_1,\dots,g_t\in B\left( e,R \right)$ with $t\in \N^{\ast}$ such that the balls $B\left( g_1,R/k \right),\dots,B\left( g_t,R/K \right)$ are disjoint, then $t\leq \left( 4k \right)^{d+1}$. That is, $t$ is finite. Taking a maximal such $t$, the balls $B\left( g_i,2R/k \right)$ cover $B\left( e,R \right)$, as else it would follow that $B\left( g,R/k \right)$ would be disjoint from all the $B\left( g_i,R/k \right)$, contradicting maximality.

  The coset map $\Gamma^{(R)}\to Y^{(R)}$ gives that the closed ball of radius $1$ and center $eN$ in $Y^{(R)}$ is covered by at most $\left( 4k \right)^{d+1}$ balls of radius $2/k$. In particular, since $Y^{(R)}$ is homogeneous and complete, we have that all closed balls of $Y$ with radius $1$ are compact (i.e., they're necessarily totally bounded), so that they are of Hausdorff dimension at most $d+1$. Thus, $Y^{(R)}$ is locally compact of dimension at most $d+1$.
\end{proof}
Therefore, by the Gleason--Montgomery--Zippin Theorem (or rather, a corollary thereof), we have that $\operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$ is a Lie group with finitely many connected components.
\section{The Action of $\Gamma$ on $Y$}%
We know that left multiplication yields a homomorphism $l\colon \Gamma^{(R)}\to \operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$, and from the previous section, that $\operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$ is a Lie group with finitely many components.. Our next job is to establish the existence of a finite-index subgroup $\Gamma'$ such that $\Gamma'$ admits homomorphisms into $\operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$ of arbitrary degree. An application of Lemma 1 will then yield the desired result.

We will assume that $l\left( \Gamma \right)\subseteq \operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$ be finite, and let $\Gamma' = \ker\left( l|_{\Gamma} \right)$. In particular, $\Gamma'$ is of finite index in $\Gamma$, and there is some finite symmetric generating set $S\subseteq \Gamma'$.
\begin{lemma}
  If $\Gamma$ has no finite index abelian subgroups, then the set of lengths $\set{\ell\left( \gamma^{-1}s\gamma \right) : s\in S,\gamma\in\Gamma'}$  is unbounded.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Otherwise, each $s$ has only finitely many $\Gamma'$-conjugates, meaning that the $C_{\Gamma'}(s)$ is finite index in $\Gamma'$. Since the center of $\Gamma'$ is the intersection of the centralizers of each of the $s\in S$, it follows that this center is finite index in $\Gamma'$, hence finite index in $\Gamma$.
\end{proof}
Recall that the group $\operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$ is topologized by the basic closed neighborhoods
\begin{align*}
  B_{k,\ve} &= \set{\sigma\in \operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right) : d\left( \sigma(y),y \right)\leq \ve\text{ for all $y$ with }d\left( y,e \right)\leq k}.
\end{align*}
at the identity for any $0 < \ve \in \R$ and $0 < k\in \N$.

\begin{proposition}
  Suppose $\Gamma$ has no abelian subgroups of finite index. Then, for each (closed) neighborhood $U$ of $\id_Y$, there is $\beta\in \left( \Gamma' \right)^{\omega}$ and $s\in S$ such that $\beta^{-1}\Gamma'\beta\subseteq \Gamma^{(R)}$ and $l\left( \beta^{-1}s\beta \right)\in U\setminus \set{\id_Y}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  For any $\gamma\in \Gamma^{\omega}$ and any $0 < r\in \R^{\ast}$, let
  \begin{align*}
    \delta\left( \gamma,r \right) &= \max\set{d\left( \gamma a,a \right) : \ell\left( a \right) \leq r}.
  \end{align*}
  We claim that
  \begin{align*}
    \delta\left( g^{-1}\gamma g,r \right) \leq \delta\left( \gamma,r \right) + 2\ell\left( g \right).
  \end{align*}
  Suppose $\ell\left( a \right)\leq r$. Then, by the left-invariance of $d$, we have
  \begin{align*}
    d\left( g^{-1}\gamma g a,a \right) &= d\left( \gamma\left( ga \right),ga \right)\\
                                       &\leq \delta\left( \gamma,\ell\left( g \right) + r \right)\\
                                       &\leq \delta\left( \gamma,r \right) + 2\ell\left( g \right),
  \end{align*}
  where the latter inequality emerges from the fact that if we write an element of $B\left( e,\ell\left( g \right) + r \right)$ as $bc$ for some $b\leq r$ and $c\leq \ell\left( g \right)$, then
  \begin{align*}
    \delta\left( \gamma bc,bc \right) &\leq d\left( \gamma bc,\gamma b \right) + d\left( \gamma b,b \right) + d\left( b,bc \right)\\
                                      &\leq d\left( bc,b \right) + d\left( \gamma b,b \right) + d\left( bc,b \right)\\
                                      &\leq \delta\left( \gamma,r \right) + 2\ell\left( g \right).
  \end{align*}
  Now, let $U = U\left( k,\ve \right)$ be a fixed neighborhood $\id$ for some $k\in \N$ and $\ve > 0$. Translating the above lemma into nonstandard language yields that we have some $s\in S$ and $g\in \left( \Gamma' \right)^{\omega}$ such that $\ell\left( g^{-1}sg \right) > \ve R$ for some infinite $R\in \R^{\ast}$.

  Write $g = s_1\cdots s_t$ for some nonstandard natural $t\in \N^{\ast}$. For any $0\leq i \leq t$, write
  \begin{align*}
    g_i &= s_1\cdots s_i\\
    M_i &= \max\set{\delta\left( g_i^{-1}sg_i,kR \right) : s\in S}.
  \end{align*}
  Let $C = \max\set{\ell\left( s \right) : s\in S}$, meaning that $C\in \N$. Notice that we have $M_0 < \ve R$ since $\Gamma'$ acts trivially on $Y$, and $M_t > \ve R$ by the assumption. Furthermore, we have $\left\vert M_{i+1}-M_i \right\vert < 2C$ for each $0\leq i \leq t-1$. Therefore, there is some $0\leq i\leq t$ such that $\left\vert M_i - \ve R \right\vert < 2C$. Define $\beta = g_i$, so that $\beta\in \left( \Gamma' \right)^{\omega}$.

  If $\gamma\in \Gamma'$, then $\beta^{-1}\gamma\beta$ is a finite product of elements of the form $\beta^{-1}s\beta$ with $s\in S$, each of which is an element of $\Gamma^{(R)}$ by how we defined $\beta$. Thus, $\beta^{-1}\Gamma'\beta\subseteq \Gamma^{(R)}$. In particular, we obtain the existence of some $s\in S$ such that $\delta\left( \beta^{-1}s\beta,kR \right)$ differs from $\ve R$ by at most $2c$. If $\sigma = l\left( \beta^{-1}s\beta \right)$, then $\sigma\neq \id$, and for any $\ell\left( a \right)\leq kR$, we have that
  \begin{align*}
    d\left( \sigma\left( aN \right),aN \right) &= \operatorname{st}\left( d\left( \sigma(a),a \right)/R \right)\\
                                               &\leq \ve,
  \end{align*}
  so that $\sigma\in U$. Thus, we obtain our desired result.
\end{proof}
Thus, we obtain the following cases.

If $l\left( \Gamma \right)$ is infinite, then we have a finite index subgroup $\Delta\subseteq \Gamma$ such that we have a nontrivial homomorphism $\Delta\to \Z$, so by the process of proving Lemma 1, it follows that $\Gamma$ virtually nilpotent.

If $l\left( \Gamma \right)$ is finite, but $\Gamma$ has an abelian subgroup of finite index, then we are done since abelian groups are nilpotent.

If $l\left( \Gamma \right)$ is finite, then $\Gamma' = \ker\left( \Gamma \right)$ has finite index in $\Gamma$. By the above proposition, $\Gamma'$ has homomorphic images in $\operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$ containing elements not equal to $\id$ that are arbitrarily close to $\id$. Since $\operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$ is a Lie group, it has the No Small Subgroups property, meaning that for each $n > 0$, there is a neighborhood of $\id$ containing no elements of order less than or equal to $n$. Thus, $\Gamma'$ has arbitrarily large homomorphic images in $\operatorname{Isom}\left( Y \right)$, so an application of Lemma 1, along with the fact that a finite index subgroup of a finite index subgroup of $\Gamma$ is finite index in $\Gamma$, once again yields our desired result.
